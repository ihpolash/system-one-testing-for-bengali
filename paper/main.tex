\begin{abstract}
\noindent Decision models answer a typed question in one forward pass and return a probability for every option.
I tested one hosted decision model, Jev, ten open decision models and one general-purpose language model on five Bengali tasks, zero-shot, in Bengali script and in Latin letters.
Jev scored highest on all five tasks among the models run on the full test sets; on Belebele it reached \jevBelebele{} (95\% CI \jevBelebeleLo{} to \jevBelebeleHi).
The open d3-flash matched or beat it on three tasks, on a subset of about \flashNmax{} items per task.
Romanization hurt every model that was above chance on Bengali script, and tool-made romanization hurt more than real typing.
On \nHuman{} items that I rewrote by hand in Banglish, Jev lost \jevLossHuman{} points against the Bengali original, where rule-based romanization of the same text cost it \jevLossRule.
The model ranking was the same either way (Kendall $\tau=\tauRule$ over \nModelsRank{} models), so synthetic romanization ranks models well but overstates how much they lose.
Every open model was overconfident on Bengali text, and one temperature fitted on a separate development set removed most of that, but less of it on romanized text.
Code, tables and the hand-written Banglish are at \url{https://github.com/ihpolash/system-one-testing-for-bengali}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A decision model reads a text and a typed question and returns one probability per option, without generating anything.
That makes the output easy to act on: a pipeline can accept confident answers and send the rest to a larger model or a person \citep{deusser2026jev,nandakishore2026laya}.
Nearly all of the evidence for this comes from English.
The one study that left English shows why that matters. On Chinese tasks Laya-multilingual scored 40.2\% where Jev scored 68.4\% \citep{wang2026chinesejev}.
For Bengali I found only the per-language Belebele and SIB-200 numbers that ship with the Jev benchmark \citep{deusser2026jev}. I found no study that asked whether a decision model's confidence still means something in Bengali.

Bengali has a second difficulty. A great deal of everyday Bengali is typed in Latin letters, with no standard spelling.
Work on other Indic languages and on Bangla dialects tests romanized text produced by a transliteration tool, and finds large losses \citep{khullar2025scriptgap,jawad2026fivedialects}.
A tool applies one rule per letter. People do not.
I could not find a measurement of how far tool-made romanization differs from what people actually type, in terms of what a model gets right.

I made that measurement for Bengali.
I rewrote \nSentences{} sentences from three datasets in Banglish by hand and scored all models on the same items in four spellings (Section~\ref{sec:human}).
Around it sits a zero-shot benchmark of eleven systems on five tasks, with calibration, risk--coverage and option-order analyses (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
Every number here is produced by the released code, and the harness reproduces published numbers from independent groups for Jev, Laya-multilingual and laya-bangla (Section~\ref{sec:audit}).
This is not the first Bengali result for any decision model, since Jev's Belebele and SIB-200 numbers exist. Kev-9B, Kev-27B, d3-27B and Microsoft-Decision-1 are not covered.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
Table~\ref{tab:gap} shows what the closest studies cover.
In English, decision models have been checked for calibration \citep{nandakishore2026laya}, for annotation work with open models \citep{ibrahim2026annotation}, for sensitivity to option names \citep{sun2026typesafe} and to option order \citep{li2026readout}, and for cost and wording robustness \citep{sharma2026typedbench}.
Other languages are thin. Chinese-Jev trains a Chinese model \citep{wang2026chinesejev}, and one paper compares Jev's cultural-value answers in English and Arabic \citep{asseri2026calibrated}.
Script robustness has been studied for generative models only \citep{khullar2025scriptgap,jawad2026fivedialects}, and the Bengali half of the large romanization corpus of \citet{gharami2025romanized} is generated by rule, not typed by people.
For calibration and selective prediction I use the standard measures \citep{guo2017calibration,geifman2017selective}; the option-rotation test follows \citet{zheng2024selectors}.
The tasks are Belebele \citep{bandarkar2023belebele}, SIB-200 \citep{adelani2023sib200}, MASSIVE \citep{fitzgerald2022massive}, SentNoB \citep{islam2021sentnob} and the Bengali translation of XNLI.

\section{Benchmark}
\label{sec:method}
\paragraph{Models.}
Jev (\texttt{jev-1.13.0}) is reached through its API.
The open models ran on Kaggle GPUs: Laya-multilingual, an mmBERT-based encoder \citep{marone2025mmbert}; laya-bangla v2, a Bangla fine-tune of it (commit \texttt{4c5eb48932}); Julia-1, with 144M parameters; the vLLM-SR Decision 3.0 models d3-edge, d3-lite, d3-nano, d3-mini and d3-flash \citep{liu2026vllmsr}; and Kev-0.8B and Kev-4B (release 1.0, tag \texttt{v1.0}), served with the project's own code in bf16 with its shipped temperature.
Kev's model cards list English as its only language and name other languages as a known weakness.
Kev-4B sits at the memory limit of a 15 GB T4, so for it I switched off CUDA-graph replay and the prefix cache, which change speed and memory but not the model, after a first run ran out of memory.
Kev was scored on the test sets and on the hand-written items only, so it is missing from the calibration and option-order results.
d3-flash has 9B parameters and did not fit comfortably, so it ran 4-bit (NF4) on a hash-ordered subset of about \flashNmax{} items per task.
Gemma-4-E4B-it is not a decision model; it serves as a baseline, with each option scored by the next-token probability of its label and the probabilities normalized over the options.
Kev-9B and Kev-27B do not fit on that GPU, and d3-27B and Microsoft-Decision-1 (API only) were not run.

\paragraph{Tasks.}
The five tasks are in Table~\ref{tab:tasks}.
Each item is a single-choice question with English instructions and option names; only the passage or utterance changes between conditions.
I cut XNLI-bn and MASSIVE to hash-ordered subsets of 1,500 items so that any smaller run is nested in a larger one.
Bengali option names changed Jev's accuracy by at most \labelMaxDiff{} points on SentNoB, XNLI-bn and SIB-200, so I kept English names throughout.
The metric is accuracy, and macro-F1 for SentNoB.

\paragraph{Text conditions.}
\emph{Bengali} is the dataset text.
\emph{Rule-based romanization} maps each letter to Latin letters by a fixed rule set, without diacritics.
\emph{Natural-style romanization} replaces each word with its most frequent spelling in a public corpus of natural Banglish (nahidstaq/bangla-transliteration-data) and falls back to rules tuned on held-out words from that corpus.
Both are synthetic. Belebele passages were not romanized.

\paragraph{Hand-written Banglish.}
I took \nSentences{} Bengali sentences from SentNoB, MASSIVE and XNLI-bn, corrected spelling errors in \nEdited{} of them, and wrote every sentence again in Banglish, the way I would type it.
All of it was written by hand, one sentence at a time, from the corrected Bengali.
Items are matched to the datasets by their original text.
An XNLI-bn item needs both sentences, so the usable set is \nHuman{} items: \nHumanMassive{} from MASSIVE, \nHumanSentnob{} from SentNoB and \nHumanNli{} XNLI-bn pairs.
Spelling correction alone moved Jev by \spellEffect{} points (95\% CI \spellLo{} to \spellHi) and no model by more than \spellMaxAny{}, so I compare the Banglish with the corrected Bengali, and I apply both romanizers to the corrected text as well.

\paragraph{Statistics.}
Intervals are 95\% percentile bootstraps over premise or passage clusters (Belebele, XNLI-bn) or over items, and over items for the paired comparisons on the hand-written set.
Calibration is measured with expected calibration error over 15 equal-width bins (ECE), the signed gap between confidence and accuracy, Brier score and risk--coverage curves.
Temperature scaling is fitted on a development split disjoint from the test items, on Bengali text only, and applied unchanged.
Option-order sensitivity uses three cyclic rotations of the options.
I report no significance tests. Comparisons are point estimates with intervals, with no correction for the number of comparisons.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Accuracy on Bengali script and romanized text}
Table~\ref{tab:main} and Figure~\ref{fig:script} give the main results.
Jev has the highest point estimate on every task among the models run on the full sets.
The closest open model is d3-nano on XNLI-bn (\accnanoXnliNat{} against \accjevXnliNat), with d3-mini at \accminiXnliNat, and the intervals in Table~\ref{tab:ci} overlap.
d3-flash, on its subset, scores \subflashXnli{} on XNLI-bn against Jev's \subjevXnli{}, and trails on Belebele (Table~\ref{tab:flash}). It is above Jev on three tasks, but with \flashNmin{} to \flashNmax{} items per task I read that as level.
Kev-4B lands next to d3-lite: \acckevmdBelNat{} on Belebele against \accliteBelNat, \acckevmdSibNat{} on SIB-200 against \accliteSibNat, \acckevmdSentNat{} on SentNoB against \accliteSentNat, and below it on XNLI-bn (\acckevmdXnliNat{} against \accliteXnliNat) and MASSIVE (\acckevmdMasNat{} against \accliteMasNat). Kev-0.8B scores \acckevsmSibNat{} on SIB-200 and \acckevsmMasNat{} on MASSIVE, and falls in the lower group with d3-edge and Laya-multilingual. Both Kev models are well below Jev on every task.
Gemma-4-E4B holds up best under romanization: on SIB-200 it keeps \accgemmaSibRom{} of \accgemmaSibNat, where d3-mini keeps \accminiSibRom{} of \accminiSibNat.
laya-bangla beats its base model on SIB-200 (\acclayabnSibNat{} against \acclayamlSibNat) and on MASSIVE (\acclayabnMasNat{} against \acclayamlMasNat) and still sits below Jev, d3-mini and Gemma-4-E4B on every task.
On SentNoB, always answering with the majority class scores a macro-F1 of \sentMajorityFone{}. Laya-multilingual reaches only \acclayamlSentNat, Kev-0.8B \acckevsmSentNat{} and Julia-1 \accjuliaSentNat. Julia-1 cannot take the 59-option MASSIVE menu at all.
Laya-multilingual's SentNoB score also depends on the wording of the question: macro-F1 is \layamlSentDefault{} with my default instruction, \layamlSentOpts{} with option names only, and \layamlSentBn{} with Bengali wording. I report the default and flag the range.

\subsection{Hand-written Banglish}
\label{sec:human}
Figure~\ref{fig:spell} and Table~\ref{tab:human} put four spellings of the same \nHuman{} items side by side.
Every model but Julia-1 does better on my Banglish than on rule-based romanization, by \hvrMin{} to \hvrMax{} points. For Jev the gain is \jevHumanVsRule{} points (95\% CI \jevHumanVsRuleLo{} to \jevHumanVsRuleHi).
Natural-style romanization lands between the two, so tuning a romanizer on real typing narrows the gap but does not close it.
The gain varies by task (Figure~\ref{fig:over}). For Jev it is \jevHumanVsRuleMassive{} points on MASSIVE (\jevHumanVsRuleMassiveLo{} to \jevHumanVsRuleMassiveHi), \jevHumanVsRuleNli{} on XNLI-bn (\jevHumanVsRuleNliLo{} to \jevHumanVsRuleNliHi, from only \nHumanNli{} pairs), and \jevHumanVsRuleSentnob{} on SentNoB (\jevHumanVsRuleSentnobLo{} to \jevHumanVsRuleSentnobHi).
My guess is that MASSIVE's short commands, full of English loan words that people spell the same way every time, are what a tool gets wrong. I did not test that.
Julia-1, near chance on these items, shows no gain.

The order of models barely moves. Kendall $\tau$ between the hand-written ordering and the rule-based one is \tauRule, and \tauNat{} for natural-style, over \nModelsRank{} models scored on the same items. That is one comparison, not eight independent ones.

\subsection{Calibration and selective prediction}
Overconfidence differs a lot between models. Raw ECE on Bengali script, averaged over XNLI-bn, MASSIVE and SentNoB, runs from \eceminiNatRaw{} for d3-mini to \ecejuliaNatRaw{} for Julia-1 (Table~\ref{tab:cal}).
One temperature, fitted on a separate Bengali development split, lowers ECE for every model on Bengali text; for Julia-1 on XNLI-bn it takes ECE from \juliaXnliEceRaw{} to \juliaXnliEceTs.
On romanized text the same temperature leaves a higher ECE for eight of the nine models with a development run (Kev and d3-flash have none). d3-mini is the exception. Jev's scaled ECE is \ecejevRomTs{} on romanized text against \ecejevNatTs{} on Bengali, and laya-bangla's is \ecelayabnRomTs{} against \ecelayabnNatTs.
Figure~\ref{fig:rel} shows the reliability curves and Figure~\ref{fig:rc} the risk--coverage curves. Confidence still ranks answers within each condition, but romanization lowers accuracy at every coverage level, including among the most confident answers.

A confidence gate set on Bengali development text to reach \gateTarget\% accuracy does not carry over.
On MASSIVE, Jev accepts \gatejevNatCov\% of Bengali items at \gatejevNatAcc\% accuracy, but only \gatejevRomCov\% of romanized items, at \gatejevRomAcc\%. d3-mini accepts \gateminiNatCov\% at \gateminiNatAcc\% and \gateminiRomCov\% at \gateminiRomAcc\%.
For laya-bangla the accepted romanized items are \gatelayabnRomAcc\% accurate, at \gatelayabnRomCov\% coverage.
So for Jev and d3-mini the gate keeps its accuracy by refusing most romanized input, and for laya-bangla it fails.
This rests on one task and, for some models, on few accepted items, so I treat it as exploratory.

\subsection{Option-order sensitivity}
Across three rotations of the options, Jev gives the same answer \consjev\% of the time, d3-nano \consnano\%, d3-lite \conslite\% and Gemma-4-E4B \consgemma\%.

\subsection{Checks on the harness}
\label{sec:audit}
Before trusting any of this I tried to reproduce other people's numbers.
From the Jev authors' released responses, Belebele ben\_Beng accuracy is \jevBelebeleZenodo; my own run gives \jevBelebele.
For Laya-multilingual I get MASSIVE \layamlNativeMassive{} and SIB-200 \layamlNativeSib, against 0.18 and 0.57 to 0.58 reported by the laya-bangla authors. For laya-bangla I get SIB-200 \layabnNativeSib{} against their 0.76.
Resending 300 Jev requests gave the same answer \jevRetestSame\% of the time, and a newer Laya package version changed no answer on 6{,}563 requests.
One comparison I could not make: laya-bangla's authors report MASSIVE 0.81 with a two-step scenario-then-intent protocol, and I ask one 59-way question and get \acclayabnMasNat.
The study used \jevRequests{} Jev requests, about \jevTokensM{}M input tokens and \$\jevUsd{} at \$0.042 per million, plus about \gpuHours{} GPU hours on Kaggle.
I measured latency under mixed conditions, so it is not comparable across models, and I do not report it.

\section{Discussion}
\paragraph{What a synthetic romanization can and cannot tell you.}
A romanization tool ranks models correctly and overstates their loss. For Jev, planning from tool-made test data would mean expecting \jevLossRule{} points lost on Banglish input, about \lossRuleVsHuman{} points too many.
I do not know why people's Banglish is easier. Two explanations fit: people spell common words the way models have seen them, and my corrections removed spelling errors that a tool copies through.
The control rules out only the second. Correcting the original Bengali moved no model by more than \spellMaxAny{} points.

\paragraph{Choosing a model.}
On Bengali script d3-mini, d3-nano and Gemma-4-E4B come within a few points of Jev on several tasks, so the hosted model's advantage here is modest.
Gemma-4-E4B is the most robust open model to romanization, and also among the worst calibrated before scaling (ECE \ecegemmaNatRaw), which temperature scaling mostly removes.
The encoders need care. Their answers change with option order, and Laya-multilingual's SentNoB score moved by more than ten points with the wording of the question.

\section{Limitations}
I wrote all of the Banglish myself, so the gap is the gap for one typing style. A second typist on a subset would show how much it varies; I have not done that.
MASSIVE supplies \pctMassive\% of the hand-written items and XNLI-bn only \nHumanNli{} pairs. XNLI-bn and MASSIVE are translations, so SentNoB is the only native Bengali text, and on SentNoB my Banglish is no easier than rule-based romanization. I do not know whether that comes from social-media text or from my sample.
d3-flash ran 4-bit on a subset, Kev ran only on the test sets and the hand-written items, the larger Kev and d3 models were not run, and Julia-1 cannot take MASSIVE.
Contamination is unknown for Jev, Gemma and the Qwen-based d3 models, and laya-bangla was trained on MASSIVE and SIB-200 training splits.
The SentNoB split I used is a third-party mirror whose provenance I could not verify.
I use one prompt format per model family, and Laya's wording sensitivity shows that this can move a result.
The calibration and gate results are exploratory and use no multiplicity correction.

\section{Conclusion}
On five Bengali tasks Jev leads, several small open models come close on Bengali script, and the open models' overconfidence is largely fixed by one temperature on Bengali text but not on romanized text.
Tool-made romanization is a fair way to rank models on Banglish input and a poor way to size their loss. To size it, test on text people wrote.
