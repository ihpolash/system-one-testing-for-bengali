\begin{abstract}
\noindent Decision models answer a typed question in one forward pass and return a probability for every option.
I tested one hosted decision model, Jev, ten open decision models and one general-purpose language model on five Bengali tasks, zero-shot, in Bengali script and in Latin letters.
Jev scored highest on all five tasks among the models run on the full test sets; on Belebele it reached \jevBelebele{} (95\% CI \jevBelebeleLo{} to \jevBelebeleHi).
The open d3-flash matched or beat it on three tasks, on a subset of about \flashNmax{} items per task.
Rule-based romanization lowered every model's accuracy on every test set where it was run.
On \nHuman{} items that ChatGPT romanized, Grok and Claude corrected and I reviewed (AI-generated Banglish), it lowered accuracy less than rule-based romanization for every model but Julia-1, and for d3-edge, Laya-multilingual and Julia-1 the loss against Bengali is within noise. Jev lost \jevLossHuman{} points against the Bengali original, where rule-based romanization of the same text cost it \jevLossRule.
The model ranking changed little between the two (Kendall $\tau=\tauFullRule$ over \nModelsRank{} models; \tauComRule{} on the \nCommon{} items that all twelve systems answered), so the size of a romanization penalty depends on how the Latin-script text is made more than the ordering of models does. None of this text was typed by a person, so how it compares with real Banglish typing is untested. The difference is concentrated in MASSIVE; on SentNoB, the only native text, it is at most \sentGainMax{} points for any model.
Every open model was overconfident on Bengali text, and one temperature fitted on a separate development set removed most of that, but less of it on rule-based romanized text.
Code, tables and the AI-generated Banglish are at \url{https://github.com/ihpolash/system-one-testing-for-bengali}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A decision model reads a text and a typed question and returns one probability per option, without generating anything.
That makes the output easy to act on: a pipeline can accept confident answers and send the rest to a larger model or a person \citep{deusser2026jev,nandakishore2026laya}.
Nearly all of the evidence for this comes from English.
The one study that left English shows why that matters. On Chinese tasks Laya-multilingual scored 40.2\% where Jev scored 68.4\% \citep{wang2026chinesejev}.
For Bengali I found only the per-language Belebele and SIB-200 numbers that ship with the Jev benchmark \citep{deusser2026jev}. I found no study that asked whether a decision model's confidence still means something in Bengali.

Bengali has a second difficulty. A great deal of everyday Bengali is typed in Latin letters, with no standard spelling.
Work on other Indic languages and on Bangla dialects tests romanized text produced by a transliteration tool, and finds large losses \citep{khullar2025scriptgap,jawad2026fivedialects}.
A tool applies one rule per letter. People do not, and a language model writes Banglish in yet another way.
I found no measurement, for Bengali, of how much of a model's loss on tool-made romanization carries over to Latin-script text produced by other means.

I measured it against one alternative: Banglish generated by ChatGPT, corrected with Grok and Claude, and reviewed by me.
I scored all models on \nSentences{} sentences from three datasets in four spellings, with that text as one of them (Section~\ref{sec:human}).
Around it sits a zero-shot benchmark of twelve systems on five tasks, with calibration, risk--coverage and option-order analyses (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
Every number here is produced by the released code, and the harness reproduces published numbers from independent groups for Jev, Laya-multilingual and laya-bangla (Section~\ref{sec:audit}).
This is not the first Bengali result for any decision model, since Jev's Belebele and SIB-200 numbers exist. Kev-9B, Kev-27B, d3-27B and Microsoft-Decision-1 are not covered.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
Table~\ref{tab:gap} shows what the closest studies cover.
In English, decision models have been checked for calibration \citep{nandakishore2026laya}, for annotation work with open models \citep{ibrahim2026annotation}, for sensitivity to option names \citep{sun2026typesafe} and to option order \citep{li2026readout}, and for cost and wording robustness \citep{sharma2026typedbench}.
Other languages are thin. Chinese-Jev trains a Chinese model \citep{wang2026chinesejev}, and one paper compares Jev's cultural-value answers in English and Arabic \citep{asseri2026calibrated}.
Script robustness has been studied for generative models only \citep{khullar2025scriptgap,jawad2026fivedialects}, and the Bengali half of the large romanization corpus of \citet{gharami2025romanized} is generated by rule, not typed by people.
For calibration and selective prediction I use the standard measures \citep{guo2017calibration,geifman2017selective}; the option-rotation test follows \citet{zheng2024selectors}.
The tasks are Belebele \citep{bandarkar2023belebele}, SIB-200 \citep{adelani2023sib200}, MASSIVE \citep{fitzgerald2022massive}, SentNoB \citep{islam2021sentnob} and the Bengali translation of XNLI.

\section{Benchmark}
\label{sec:method}
\paragraph{Models.}
Jev (\texttt{jev-1.13.0}) is reached through its API.
The open models ran on Kaggle GPUs: Laya-multilingual, an mmBERT-based encoder \citep{marone2025mmbert}; laya-bangla v2, a Bangla fine-tune of it (commit \texttt{4c5eb48932}); Julia-1, with 144M parameters; the vLLM-SR Decision 3.0 models d3-edge, d3-lite, d3-nano, d3-mini and d3-flash \citep{liu2026vllmsr}; and Kev-0.8B and Kev-4B (release 1.0, tag \texttt{v1.0}), served with the project's own code in bf16 with its shipped temperature.
Kev's model cards list English as its only language and name other languages as a known weakness.
Kev-4B sits at the memory limit of a 15 GB T4, so for it I switched off CUDA-graph replay and the prefix cache, which change speed and memory but not the model, after a first run ran out of memory.
Kev was scored on the test sets and on the AI-generated Banglish items only, so it is missing from the calibration and option-order results.
d3-flash has 9B parameters and did not fit comfortably, so it ran 4-bit (NF4) on a hash-ordered subset of about \flashNmax{} items per task.
Gemma-4-E4B-it is not a decision model; it serves as a baseline, with each option scored by the next-token probability of its label and the probabilities normalized over the options.
Kev-9B and Kev-27B do not fit on that GPU, and d3-27B and Microsoft-Decision-1 (API only) were not run.

\paragraph{Tasks.}
The five tasks are in Table~\ref{tab:tasks}.
Each item is a single-choice question with English instructions and option names; only the passage or utterance changes between conditions. The exact question structure for every task is in \texttt{docs/PROMPTS.md} in the repository.
I cut XNLI-bn and MASSIVE to hash-ordered subsets of 1,500 items so that any smaller run is nested in a larger one.
Bengali option names changed Jev's accuracy by at most \labelMaxDiff{} points on SentNoB, XNLI-bn and SIB-200, so I kept English names throughout.
The metric is accuracy, and macro-F1 for SentNoB.

\paragraph{Text conditions.}
\emph{Bengali} is the dataset text.
\emph{Rule-based romanization} maps each letter to Latin letters by a fixed rule set, without diacritics.
\emph{Natural-style romanization} replaces each word with its most frequent spelling in a public corpus of natural Banglish (nahidstaq/bangla-transliteration-data) and falls back to rules tuned on held-out words from that corpus.
Both are synthetic. Belebele passages were not romanized.

\paragraph{AI-generated Banglish.}
I took \nSentences{} Bengali sentences from SentNoB, MASSIVE and XNLI-bn; spelling errors in \nEdited{} of them were corrected before romanization.
ChatGPT (Luna 6.1) generated a Banglish rendering of every sentence. Grok (4.7) and Claude (Sonnet 5.5) were then used to correct those renderings, and I supervised and reviewed the corrections; about 35\% of the renderings were changed. I corrected the spelling of the Bengali source myself.
No sentence was typed by a person from scratch, so this condition is AI-generated, author-reviewed romanization, not a sample of how people type, and I call it AI-generated Banglish throughout.
I did not record the prompts. The repository holds a reference prompt of the same kind (\texttt{docs/BANGLISH\_GENERATION.md}), written afterwards and not the one used.
Items are matched to the datasets by their original text.
An XNLI-bn item needs both sentences, so the usable set is \nHuman{} items: \nHumanMassive{} from MASSIVE, \nHumanSentnob{} from SentNoB and \nHumanNli{} XNLI-bn pairs. No sentence was dropped; Table~\ref{tab:flow} shows how \nSentences{} sentences become \nHuman{} items.
Spelling correction alone moved Jev by \spellEffect{} points (95\% CI \spellLo{} to \spellHi) and no model by more than \spellMaxAny{}, so I compare the AI-generated Banglish with the corrected Bengali, and I apply both romanizers to the corrected text as well.

\paragraph{Statistics.}
Intervals are 95\% percentile bootstraps (seed 0). On the test sets they resample premise or passage clusters (Belebele, XNLI-bn) or items, 1,000 times (400 for the SentNoB macro-F1, 500 for paired differences). On the \nHuman{} AI-generated Banglish items they resample items, 1,000 times, and every comparison between conditions is a paired difference on the same items.
Calibration is measured with expected calibration error over 15 equal-width bins (ECE), the signed gap between confidence and accuracy, the Brier score and risk--coverage curves. I report two ECE aggregations and they differ on purpose. The primary one is computed within each task on the full test sets and then averaged without weights over XNLI-bn, MASSIVE and SentNoB (Table~\ref{tab:cal}; per task in Table~\ref{tab:caltasks}). The other pools the \nHuman{} items across tasks (Table~\ref{tab:calitems}), so MASSIVE weighs \pctMassive\% of it; it is the only calibration measure available for the AI-generated Banglish, and the two should not be compared number for number.
Temperature scaling uses one scalar per model and task. It minimizes the mean negative log-likelihood over a grid of 41 log-spaced temperatures from 0.2 to 6, on a development split of 1,500 items (XNLI-bn, MASSIVE, SentNoB) that is disjoint from the test items and holds Bengali text only. The fitted temperature is then applied unchanged to the test items in Bengali and in rule-based romanization; no test label enters the fit. Belebele and SIB-200 have no development split, and d3-flash and Kev were not run on one, so they have no scaled results. The confidence gate uses the same development split: its threshold is the smallest one whose accepted dev accuracy reaches \gateTarget\%, with at least 5\% coverage.
Option-order sensitivity uses three cyclic rotations of the options.
I report no significance tests. The main comparison is AI-generated Banglish against rule-based romanization on the pooled items; every other comparison is descriptive, and none is corrected for the number of comparisons.

\subsection{Accuracy on Bengali script and romanized text}
Table~\ref{tab:main} and Figure~\ref{fig:script} give the main results.
Jev has the highest point estimate on every task among the models run on the full sets.
The closest open model is d3-nano on XNLI-bn (\accnanoXnliNat{} against \accjevXnliNat), with d3-mini at \accminiXnliNat, and the intervals in Table~\ref{tab:ci} overlap.
d3-flash, on its subset, scores \subflashXnli{} on XNLI-bn against Jev's \subjevXnli{}, and trails on Belebele (Table~\ref{tab:flash}). It is above Jev on three tasks, but with \flashNmin{} to \flashNmax{} items per task I read that as level.
Kev-4B lands next to d3-lite: \acckevmdBelNat{} on Belebele against \accliteBelNat, \acckevmdSibNat{} on SIB-200 against \accliteSibNat, \acckevmdSentNat{} on SentNoB against \accliteSentNat, and below it on XNLI-bn (\acckevmdXnliNat{} against \accliteXnliNat) and MASSIVE (\acckevmdMasNat{} against \accliteMasNat). Kev-0.8B scores \acckevsmSibNat{} on SIB-200 and \acckevsmMasNat{} on MASSIVE, and falls in the lower group with d3-edge and Laya-multilingual. Both Kev models are well below Jev on every task.
Gemma-4-E4B holds up best under romanization: on SIB-200 it keeps \accgemmaSibRom{} of \accgemmaSibNat, where d3-mini keeps \accminiSibRom{} of \accminiSibNat.
laya-bangla beats its base model on SIB-200 (\acclayabnSibNat{} against \acclayamlSibNat) and on MASSIVE (\acclayabnMasNat{} against \acclayamlMasNat) and still sits below Jev, d3-mini and Gemma-4-E4B on every task.

Laya-multilingual's SentNoB score also depends on the wording of the question: macro-F1 is \layamlSentDefault{} with my default instruction, \layamlSentOpts{} with option names only, and \layamlSentBn{} with Bengali wording. I report the default and flag the range.

\subsection{AI-generated Banglish}
\label{sec:human}
Figure~\ref{fig:spell} and Table~\ref{tab:human} put four spellings of the same \nHuman{} items side by side. The pooled figures are plain accuracy over three tasks with different numbers of options, so they describe this item set and are not a cross-task quality score; the per-task comparisons in Table~\ref{tab:tasks2} are the primary evidence.
Every model but Julia-1 does better on the AI-generated Banglish than on rule-based romanization, by \hvrMin{} to \hvrMax{} points. For Jev the gain is \jevHumanVsRule{} points (95\% CI \jevHumanVsRuleLo{} to \jevHumanVsRuleHi).
Natural-style romanization lands between the two, so tuning a romanizer on typed Banglish narrows the gap but does not close it.
The gain varies by task (Figure~\ref{fig:over}). For Jev it is \jevHumanVsRuleMassive{} points on MASSIVE (\jevHumanVsRuleMassiveLo{} to \jevHumanVsRuleMassiveHi), \jevHumanVsRuleNli{} on XNLI-bn (\jevHumanVsRuleNliLo{} to \jevHumanVsRuleNliHi, from only \nHumanNli{} pairs), and \jevHumanVsRuleSentnob{} on SentNoB (\jevHumanVsRuleSentnobLo{} to \jevHumanVsRuleSentnobHi).
Because MASSIVE makes up \pctMassive\% of the pooled items, Table~\ref{tab:tasks2} gives each task separately and the unweighted mean of the three task differences. For Jev that mean is \balJev{} points, and across the models other than Julia-1 it runs from \balMin{} to \balMax{}, so the gain does not come from the weighting. It does come mostly from MASSIVE. On SentNoB the interval for the gain includes zero for six of the twelve systems, and for Julia-1 it is negative.
My guess is that MASSIVE's short commands, full of English loan words, are what a rule-based tool gets wrong and a language model spells in the conventional way. I did not test that.
Julia-1, near chance on these items, shows no gain.

The order of models moves little, but how little depends on the items. Over the \nModelsRank{} models that answered all \nFull{} items (every system but Julia-1), Kendall $\tau$ between the ordering on AI-generated Banglish and on rule-based romanization is \tauFullRule{} (95\% bootstrap interval \tauFullRuleLo{} to \tauFullRuleHi), and \tauFullNat{} (\tauFullNatLo{} to \tauFullNatHi) for natural-style. On the \nCommon{} items that all \nSystemsCommon{} systems answered, which excludes MASSIVE because Julia-1 cannot take it, the values are \tauComRule{} (\tauComRuleLo{} to \tauComRuleHi) and \tauComNat{} (\tauComNatLo{} to \tauComNatHi). The agreement is clearly positive and clearly short of identical. The systems are scored on the same items, so this is one comparison, not eleven independent ones.

\subsection{Calibration and selective prediction}
Overconfidence differs a lot between models. Raw ECE on Bengali script, averaged over the three tasks as described above, runs from \eceminiNatRaw{} for d3-mini to \ecejuliaNatRaw{} for Julia-1 (Table~\ref{tab:cal}).
One temperature, fitted on a separate Bengali development split, lowers ECE for every model on Bengali text; for Julia-1 on XNLI-bn it takes ECE from \juliaXnliEceRaw{} to \juliaXnliEceTs.
On rule-based romanized text the same temperature leaves a higher ECE for eight of the nine models with a development run (Kev and d3-flash have none). d3-mini is the exception. Jev's scaled ECE is \ecejevRomTs{} on romanized text against \ecejevNatTs{} on Bengali, and laya-bangla's is \ecelayabnRomTs{} against \ecelayabnNatTs.
Figure~\ref{fig:rel} shows the reliability curves and Figure~\ref{fig:rc} the risk--coverage curves. Confidence still ranks answers within each condition, but rule-based romanization lowers accuracy at every coverage level, including among the most confident answers.
Without any scaling, on the \nHuman{} items, raw ECE (Table~\ref{tab:calitems}) stays within 0.05 across the three conditions for Jev (\eceItemsjevBn{}, \eceItemsjevRule{} and \eceItemsjevAi{} for Bengali, rule-based and AI-generated Banglish), d3-mini and the two Kev models. For Gemma-4-E4B, d3-nano, d3-lite, laya-bangla, d3-edge and Laya-multilingual it is highest under rule-based romanization, with AI-generated Banglish in between. Julia-1 is near chance and uninformative here. So the miscalibration under romanization is real for most models but not for all, and I did not fit a temperature for the AI-generated condition.

A confidence gate set on Bengali development text to reach \gateTarget\% accuracy does not carry over.
On MASSIVE, Jev accepts \gatejevNatCov\% of Bengali items (\gatejevNatN{} items) at \gatejevNatAcc\% accuracy, but only \gatejevRomCov\% of rule-based romanized items (\gatejevRomN{}), at \gatejevRomAcc\%. d3-mini accepts \gateminiNatCov\% (\gateminiNatN{}) at \gateminiNatAcc\% and \gateminiRomCov\% (\gateminiRomN{}) at \gateminiRomAcc\%.
For laya-bangla the accepted rule-based romanized items are \gatelayabnRomAcc\% accurate, but only \gatelayabnRomN{} items are accepted, so that figure is close to anecdote.
So for Jev and d3-mini the gate keeps its accuracy by refusing most rule-based romanized input, and for laya-bangla it fails.
This rests on one task and, for some models, on few accepted items, so I treat it as exploratory.

\subsection{Checks on the harness}
\label{sec:audit}
Before trusting any of this I tried to reproduce other people's numbers.
From the Jev authors' released responses, Belebele ben\_Beng accuracy is \jevBelebeleZenodo; my own run gives \jevBelebele.
For Laya-multilingual I get MASSIVE \layamlNativeMassive{} and SIB-200 \layamlNativeSib, against 0.18 and 0.57 to 0.58 reported by the laya-bangla authors. For laya-bangla I get SIB-200 \layabnNativeSib{} against their 0.76.
Resending 300 Jev requests gave the same answer \jevRetestSame\% of the time, and a newer Laya package version changed no answer on 6{,}563 requests.
One comparison I could not make: laya-bangla's authors report MASSIVE 0.81 with a two-step scenario-then-intent protocol, and I ask one 59-way question and get \acclayabnMasNat.
The study used \jevRequests{} Jev requests, about \jevTokensM{}M input tokens and \$\jevUsd{} at \$0.042 per million, plus about \gpuHours{} GPU hours on Kaggle.
I measured latency under mixed conditions, so it is not comparable across models, and I do not report it.

\section{Discussion}
\paragraph{What a rule-based romanization can and cannot tell you.}
Rule-based romanization ranks models in nearly the same order as AI-generated Banglish, and costs them more accuracy. For Jev, test data from the rule-based romanizer would suggest losing \jevLossRule{} points on Banglish input, about \lossRuleVsHuman{} points more than on the AI-generated text.
That does not say which of the two is closer to what people type. A language model's Banglish may be cleaner than real typing, and closer to the web text the models were trained on, which would make the loss on real input larger than I measured. What the comparison does show is that the size of a romanization penalty depends on how the Latin-script text is made, so a number measured on one procedure should not be carried over to another.
I do not know why the AI-generated text is easier than the rule-based text. Two explanations fit: common words are spelled the way models have seen them, and the spelling corrections removed errors that a tool copies through.
The control rules out only the second. Correcting the original Bengali moved no model by more than \spellMaxAny{} points.

\paragraph{Choosing a model.}
On Bengali script d3-mini, d3-nano and Gemma-4-E4B come within a few points of Jev on several tasks, so the hosted model's advantage here is modest.
Gemma-4-E4B is the most robust open model to romanization, and also among the worst calibrated before scaling (ECE \ecegemmaNatRaw), which temperature scaling mostly removes.
The encoders need care. Their answers change with option order, and Laya-multilingual's SentNoB score moved by more than ten points with the wording of the question.

\section{Limitations}
The Banglish was generated by ChatGPT and corrected with Grok and Claude under my supervision, so this evaluation measures AI-generated, author-reviewed romanization, not text typed by several Bengali speakers.
The final spellings may reflect the conventions of those models, and because language-model text may sit close to the data that language models are trained on, it may be easier than real typing for some of the systems I tested.
Matching the items controls for item identity; it cannot separate human typing from AI generation. Banglish collected independently from several writers under one protocol is the test of whether these differences generalise, and I have not done that.
MASSIVE supplies \pctMassive\% of the items and XNLI-bn only \nHumanNli{} pairs. XNLI-bn and MASSIVE are translations, so SentNoB is the only native Bengali text, and on SentNoB the AI-generated Banglish is no easier than rule-based romanization for several models. I do not know whether that comes from social-media text or from the generation procedure.
d3-flash ran 4-bit on a subset, Kev ran only on the test sets and the AI-generated Banglish items, the larger Kev and d3 models were not run, and Julia-1 cannot take MASSIVE.
Contamination is unknown for Jev, Gemma and the Qwen-based d3 models, and laya-bangla was trained on MASSIVE and SIB-200 training splits.
The SentNoB split I used is a third-party mirror whose provenance I could not verify.
I use one prompt format per model family, and Laya's wording sensitivity shows that this can move a result.
The calibration and gate results are exploratory and use no multiplicity correction.

\section{Conclusion}
On five Bengali tasks Jev leads, several small open models come close on Bengali script, and the open models' overconfidence is largely fixed by one temperature on Bengali text but less fully on rule-based romanized text.
Rule-based romanization ranks models in nearly the same order as AI-generated Banglish but costs them more accuracy. A loss measured on one romanization procedure should not be carried to another, and Banglish typed independently by several people is needed to say which procedure matches real use.
